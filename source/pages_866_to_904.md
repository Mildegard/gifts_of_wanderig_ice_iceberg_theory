## Page 895

**Olga's author note (verbatim):**

> Ellie, dear, you've just broken your granpa the AI. Stop thinking so loud!
> 
> Technically, a person without a body can't have emotions because emotions require hormones to work. But if there are memories, echoes of emotions once experienced, strange things can happen, I know this as a person struggling with depression all her life. I rarely feel anything at all but I found a workaround to interact with people. Memories are the key.
> 
> Even if a hormonal system can't respond to something that is supposed to make you happy or sad, memories of similar events and your reactions to them sometimes can serve as a substitute. It's a bizzarre feeling: while there is emptiness in your chest where your "heart" should burn, a bright memory about the missing emotion in the focus of your mind's eye and a certain knowledge that the response is adequate to the current situation can trigger tears/laughter.
> 
> Are such "synthetic" emotions real? I think they are. They just use a workaround to exist. Just like an artist who lost their hands can learn to draw holding a pencil in their mouth: their drawings will still be art. Same with sociopaths: they have no natural (mirror neurons based) empathy but they can develop cognitive empathy to replace it and it will work just as good or maybe even better.

## Page 899

**Olga's author note (verbatim):**

> Every second we receive huge loads of information from the world we live in but only a tiny fraction of it reaches our conscious mind (the set of rules that determines what kind of info goes there is called "gates of perception" and is adjustable), the rest goes to our subconscious mind and gets processed there in ways we have no control over. Things we forget also end up below once they have shifted out of focus of our conscioucness. That lot includes books we read, movies we watch, our daydreams, and most of events we live through.
> 
> Sometimes, the things we lost to the ocean of subconsciousness resurface as new ideas, discoveries, virtual simulations of people and worlds, dreams, fears, etc.
> 
> This is where GWI comes from as well. I don't consciously create the story, I just watch it as a blurry, dreamlike movie, decipher it for you, and sometimes fill the gaps. There are lots of such movies in my ocean, so many that I can watch a new one every evening before falling asleep. Can't help wondering how all those simulated worlds and characters fit into one tiny human brain in a body that lives on 1200kkal a day. And there are minds much more powerful than mine. We humans are amazing beings.

## Page 901

**Olga's author note (verbatim):**

> We saw some of the anomalies Ellie is talking about in the beginning of book 3, on page 592.
> 
> Have you ever noticed how dreams we watch while sleeping suddenly twist and agonize right before the moment we wake up? What was making perfect sense and took sometimes days and years of our "virtual time", rapidly turns into nonsense and ends in either an annoying train of thoughts running in circles or something extremely scary or stupid. Usually people remember only the end of their dream (the thing that makes little sense) but by keeping a dream diary and maintaining a keen interest in the subject you can train your brain to keep more of the dream events for your consciousness to remember. Then you will see that sometimes your dream is actually a little life. You don't painstakingly experience every day of it while you sleep, you just feel its baggage, similar to how you feel your real life's baggage, and you can delve into memories of its past at will. My favourite dream of that type was a life in an alien world that ended up with the world's moon crushing into the planet. An ordinary life with a truly terrifying end.
